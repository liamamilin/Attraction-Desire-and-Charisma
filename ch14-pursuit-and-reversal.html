14  Pursuit and Reversal

14.1 The Dynamics of the Chase

Attraction is rarely symmetrical from the start. More commonly, one person is more attracted than the other. One person pursues; the other is pursued. This asymmetry creates a particular dynamic — the chase — that shapes the emotional experience of both parties.

But asymmetry is not permanent. Attraction can shift. The pursuer can become the pursued. The pursued can become the pursuer. These reversals are among the most dramatic and consequential events in the architecture of attraction. This chapter examines how pursuit works, why reversals happen, and what they mean.

14.2 The Psychology of Pursuit

14.2.1 The Pursuer

The pursuer is the person who experiences stronger attraction and acts on it. They initiate contact, express interest, seek closeness, and invest emotional energy in moving the relationship forward.

Pursuit is motivated by desire, but it is also shaped by psychology. Some people are chronic pursuers — they are almost always the more attracted party, almost always the one initiating and investing. This pattern often reflects:

  • Anxious attachment: the fear of abandonment drives constant pursuit of reassurance and connection
  • Low self-worth: the belief that one must earn love through effort rather than being worthy of it inherently
  • Idealization: the tendency to project extraordinary value onto others, making them seem worth pursuing intensely
  • Scarcity mindset: the belief that desirable partners are rare and must be pursued aggressively before they are lost

Chronic pursuit is exhausting and often counterproductive. The pursuer’s intensity can feel like pressure, which triggers withdrawal in the pursued. The pursuer’s availability can reduce their perceived value. The pursuer’s emotional investment can create vulnerability to manipulation.

14.2.2 The Pursued

The pursued is the person who receives pursuit. They may be attracted to the pursuer or not; the defining feature is that they are the object of stronger attraction and do not need to initiate or invest to the same degree.

Being pursued can feel wonderful — it is validating to be wanted. But it can also feel uncomfortable. Unwanted pursuit feels like pressure or invasion. Even wanted pursuit can feel like a burden if the pursuer’s intensity exceeds the pursued’s comfort level.

The pursued has a structural advantage in the dynamic: they control the pace. They can accelerate or decelerate. They can accept or reject. This control is part of what makes the pursued position attractive — it involves less vulnerability and more agency.

But the pursued position also has costs. The pursued may feel guilty about their advantage. They may doubt whether the pursuer’s attraction is genuine or merely a product of the chase dynamic. They may feel pressure to reciprocate feelings they do not have.

14.3 The Reversal

A reversal occurs when the asymmetry of attraction flips: the pursuer loses interest and the pursued becomes interested, or the pursued’s interest grows to exceed the pursuer’s.

14.3.1 How Reversals Happen

The pursuer stops pursuing. When the pursuer withdraws — whether from exhaustion, loss of interest, or strategic decision — the dynamic changes. The pursued, accustomed to being the object of pursuit, experiences the withdrawal as a loss. The validation that pursuit provided disappears. The mystery of why the pursuer stopped creates curiosity. The pursued may begin to pursue.

This is the mechanism behind the common advice to “play hard to get.” By withdrawing pursuit, you create the conditions for reversal. The pursued, who was comfortable in their position, suddenly feels the absence of desire and may respond by generating their own.

The pursued’s interest grows. Sometimes reversal happens because the pursued’s attraction genuinely increases over time. As they get to know the pursuer, as they experience the pursuer’s qualities, as their own projections develop, their desire catches up to or exceeds the pursuer’s.

This is the healthier form of reversal. It is based on genuine development of attraction rather than on the psychological dynamics of withdrawal and loss.

The pursuer’s value increases. If the pursuer’s perceived value rises — through achievement, social proof, or changed circumstances — the pursued’s attraction may increase correspondingly. The person who seemed ordinary becomes extraordinary, and the dynamic reverses.

The triangle effect. When a third party enters the dynamic — someone else who desires the pursuer or the pursued — attraction can intensify and reverse. This is the subject of the next chapter.

14.3.2 The Psychology of Reversal

Reversal is psychologically complex. For the former pursuer, being pursued can feel validating but also disorienting. The dynamic they were accustomed to has changed. The person they were chasing is now chasing them. This can be satisfying, but it can also reveal that the attraction was more about the chase than the person.

For the former pursued, becoming the pursuer can feel vulnerable. They are now in the position of wanting more than they are wanted, of risking rejection, of investing without guarantee of return. This vulnerability can be uncomfortable, especially for people accustomed to the safety of the pursued position.

14.4 The Pursuit Paradox

There is a deep paradox in pursuit dynamics: pursuit often undermines its own goal. The more intensely someone pursues, the more they may push the other person away. The more available they make themselves, the less valuable they may seem. The more they invest, the more vulnerable they become.

This paradox does not mean that pursuit is always counterproductive. Appropriate pursuit — expressing interest clearly but without pressure, investing proportionally to the other person’s investment, maintaining one’s own center while moving toward another — can be effective and attractive.

The paradox applies to excessive, anxious, or desperate pursuit. When pursuit communicates need rather than desire, dependency rather than choice, it becomes unattractive. The pursued senses that they are being pursued not because they are genuinely valued but because the pursuer needs someone — anyone — to fill an emotional void.

14.5 Navigating Pursuit and Reversal

14.5.1 If You Are the Pursuer

Pursue with confidence, not desperation. Communicate your interest clearly but without pressure. “I enjoy spending time with you and would like to do more of it” is confident. “I can’t stop thinking about you and need to know how you feel” is desperate.

Match investment levels. Invest proportionally to the other person’s investment. If you are always the one initiating, always the one giving, always the one accommodating, pull back. Let the other person invest too. Mutual investment builds mutual attraction.

Maintain your own life. The pursuer who abandons their own interests, friendships, and pursuits to focus entirely on the pursued loses the independence that makes them attractive. Maintain your own center. The pursuit should be one dimension of your life, not your entire life.

Know when to stop. Not every pursuit succeeds. If the other person is consistently unresponsive, consistently unavailable, or consistently clear about their lack of interest, stop. Continuing to pursue in the face of clear rejection is not romantic; it is disrespectful.

14.5.2 If You Are the Pursued

Be clear about your feelings. If you are not interested, say so clearly and kindly. Ambiguity may feel kinder than rejection, but it prolongs the pursuer’s uncertainty and investment, which is ultimately more painful.

Don’t exploit the dynamic. The structural advantage of the pursued position can be tempting to exploit — enjoying the validation, the attention, the favors without any intention of reciprocating. This is using another person, and it causes real harm.

Examine your own attraction. If you find yourself only attracted to people who pursue you intensely, examine why. Is it because you genuinely like them, or because the pursuit provides validation you are not giving yourself? Attraction based on being pursued is fragile — it depends on the pursuit continuing, and pursuit rarely continues indefinitely.

14.6 From Pursuit to Triangles
