1  What Is Attraction?

1.1 The Definition Problem

Ask ten people what attraction means and you will receive ten different answers. A neuroscientist might talk about dopamine and the ventral tegmental area. A poet might speak of lightning strikes and gravitational pulls. A marketing executive might describe the art of making products desirable. A teenager might simply say, “You know it when you feel it.”

This definitional ambiguity is not a sign that attraction is mysterious beyond comprehension. It is a sign that attraction is not one thing. It is a family of related phenomena that share a common structure — emotional movement toward an object — but differ in their mechanisms, intensities, and objects.

Before we can analyze attraction productively, we need to clarify what we mean by the term. This chapter proposes a working definition and distinguishes attraction from neighboring concepts: persuasion, liking, respect, dependency, and love.

1.2 Attraction as Emotional Movement

The core of attraction is movement. When you are attracted to someone, your emotional attention moves toward them. You think about them when they are not present. You orient your body toward them when they are in the room. You feel a pull — sometimes gentle, sometimes urgent — to be closer, to know more, to be known.

This movement has three essential features:

Directionality. Attraction is always toward something. It has an object. Unlike a diffuse mood like anxiety or contentment, attraction is organized around a specific person, thing, or idea. This directionality is what makes attraction different from general arousal or excitement.

Asymmetry. Attraction is not necessarily mutual. Person A can be attracted to Person B without Person B being attracted to Person A. This asymmetry is a fundamental feature of attraction and the source of much of its drama. It also means that attraction is a property of the attracted person’s psychology, not of the attractive person’s qualities. Qualities may trigger attraction, but attraction itself happens inside the person who feels it.

Motivation. Attraction is not merely a cognitive judgment (“that person is attractive”) but a motivational state (“I want to move toward that person”). It involves desire, approach motivation, and behavioral inclination. You can recognize that someone is conventionally beautiful without feeling attracted to them. Attraction requires the motivational component.

Putting these together: Attraction is the directional, asymmetrical, motivational movement of emotional attention toward a person.

1.3 What Attraction Is Not

To sharpen the definition, it helps to distinguish attraction from concepts with which it is often confused.

1.3.1 Attraction vs. Persuasion

Persuasion is the process of changing someone’s beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors through communication. A salesperson persuades you to buy a product. A lawyer persuades a jury. A friend persuades you to try a new restaurant.

Attraction and persuasion overlap — attractive people are often more persuasive, and persuasive people can become attractive — but they are fundamentally different processes. Persuasion operates through argument, evidence, and rhetorical appeal. Attraction operates through emotional movement. You can be persuaded by someone you are not attracted to, and you can be attracted to someone who cannot persuade you of anything.

The distinction matters because confusing attraction with persuasion leads people to try to argue their way into being attractive. They explain why they would be a good partner, list their qualities, make a case for themselves. This almost never works, because attraction is not a conclusion reached through reasoning. It is an emotional movement that reasoning can influence but not produce.

1.3.2 Attraction vs. Liking

Liking is a positive evaluation. You like someone when you find them pleasant, agreeable, or enjoyable to be around. Liking is warm. It is comfortable. It is the feeling you have toward a good colleague, a friendly neighbor, or a pleasant acquaintance.

Attraction is hotter than liking. It involves tension, desire, and a sense of incompleteness. You can like someone without being attracted to them, and you can be attracted to someone you do not particularly like. The classic example is the charismatic but unpleasant person who draws attention and desire despite being difficult to be around.

The distinction between liking and attraction explains a common romantic frustration: the “nice person” who cannot understand why their niceness does not generate attraction. Niceness produces liking, but attraction requires additional elements — tension, mystery, perceived value, emotional movement. Liking without these elements is friendship material, not romantic material.

1.3.3 Attraction vs. Respect

Respect is admiration for someone’s qualities, achievements, or character. You respect a mentor, a skilled artist, a person of integrity. Respect involves looking up to someone, recognizing their superiority in some domain.

Respect can contribute to attraction — we are often attracted to people we admire — but respect alone is not attraction. You can deeply respect someone without wanting to move toward them emotionally or physically. And you can be attracted to someone you do not respect at all, which is the basis of the “bad boy” or “femme fatale” archetype: attraction organized around danger, excitement, and transgression rather than admiration.

1.3.4 Attraction vs. Dependency

Dependency is a state of needing someone for practical or emotional support. A child depends on a parent. A patient depends on a doctor. An emotionally fragile person depends on a partner for stability.

Dependency can masquerade as attraction. When someone provides security, validation, or practical support that you feel you cannot provide for yourself, you may experience a powerful pull toward them. But this pull is not attraction in the sense we are analyzing. It is need. The distinction becomes clear when the dependency is removed: if the feeling disappears when you no longer need the person, it was dependency, not attraction.

Genuine attraction persists even when you do not need the person. You want them, but you do not depend on them for your psychological survival. This is one reason why self-sufficiency is paradoxically attractive: it signals that any attraction you feel is genuine desire, not need.

1.3.5 Attraction vs. Love

Love is a broader, deeper, and more enduring emotional bond. It includes care, commitment, attachment, and a willingness to sacrifice for the other person’s wellbeing. Love can exist without the acute tension of attraction, and attraction can exist without the depth of love.

The relationship between attraction and love is complex. Attraction often initiates relationships that develop into love. Love can sustain relationships after the initial intensity of attraction fades. And attraction can reappear within love, creating cycles of renewed desire. But they are distinct phenomena, and confusing them leads to disappointment — expecting the sustained intensity of early attraction to persist throughout a loving relationship, or expecting love to generate attraction when the conditions for attraction are absent.

1.4 The Dimensions of Attraction

Attraction is not a single dimension from “not attracted” to “very attracted.” It has multiple dimensions that can vary independently.

Intensity. How strong is the pull? Mild attraction is a pleasant inclination; intense attraction is an obsession. Intensity is influenced by novelty, uncertainty, perceived value, and the presence of obstacles.

Quality. What kind of pull is it? Attraction can feel exciting, calming, destabilizing, inspiring, or consuming. The same person can generate different qualities of attraction in different people, depending on what each person projects onto them.

Durability. How long does the attraction last? Some attractions flare intensely and fade quickly. Others build slowly and persist for years. Durability is influenced by the depth of connection, the presence of ongoing mystery, and whether the attraction is based on fantasy or reality.

Specificity. How narrow is the attraction? Some people are attracted to a wide range of people; others are attracted only to very specific types. Specificity reflects the particular emotional architecture of each person — what they find moving, what they project, what they desire.

Consciousness. How aware is the person of their attraction? Some attractions are fully conscious and acknowledged. Others are denied, repressed, or experienced as something else — irritation, fascination, competitiveness. Unconscious attraction can be especially powerful because it operates outside rational control.

1.5 The Attraction System

The central argument of this book is that attraction is not a single emotion but a system of interacting dynamics. When we say someone is “attractive,” we are making a shorthand summary of a complex process: they capture attention, generate curiosity, stimulate desire, invite projection, maintain mystery, manage distance, create tension, and signal value.

Each of these dynamics will be explored in detail in the chapters ahead. For now, the key insight is that attraction is compositional. It is not a thing you have or don’t have; it is a pattern you participate in. Understanding the pattern gives you more agency within it — not control over others, but understanding of yourself and the dynamics you are part of.
