15  Triangles, Rivalry, and Social Desire

15.1 The Third Point

Attraction is often imagined as a line between two people. But much of the time, there is a third point — another person, a social context, an audience — that shapes the dynamic. Triangles are not aberrations in the architecture of attraction; they are common, powerful, and psychologically revealing.

This chapter examines how third parties influence attraction: how rivalry intensifies desire, how social proof amplifies attractiveness, how jealousy operates, and how triangles can be navigated without causing harm.

15.2 The Triangular Structure

A triangle in attraction is any configuration where a third party influences the dynamic between two people. The third party may be:

  • A rival: someone who also desires one of the two people
  • An alternative: someone one of the two people could be with instead
  • An observer: an audience whose attention or judgment matters
  • A former partner: someone from the past whose presence shapes the present
  • An idealized other: someone who exists in fantasy rather than reality but still influences the dynamic

Triangles can be explicit (everyone knows about everyone) or implicit (the third party exists in one person’s mind but not in the shared reality). They can be symmetrical (both people have alternatives) or asymmetrical (only one does). They can be stable (the triangle persists over time) or unstable (it resolves into a dyad or dissolves entirely).

15.3 Why Triangles Intensify Attraction

15.3.1 Competition and Scarcity

When someone is desired by others, they seem more valuable. This is the principle of social proof applied to attraction: if other people want this person, there must be something worth wanting. The competition creates perceived scarcity, and scarcity increases value.

This effect is not purely rational. The knowledge that someone has other options can trigger a visceral intensification of desire. The person who seemed merely interesting becomes urgently desirable when you realize they might choose someone else.

15.3.2 The Rivalry Dynamic

Rivalry adds an element of competition that engages different psychological systems than pure attraction. The desire to win, to prevail over a rival, to prove oneself superior — these competitive motivations can amplify and sometimes replace genuine attraction.

This is why people sometimes pursue someone more intensely when a rival appears, even if their underlying attraction has not changed. The rivalry has made the pursuit into a competition, and the competition is motivating in its own right.

The danger is that rivalry-based attraction often collapses when the rivalry resolves. If the motivation was primarily competitive, winning feels satisfying but the attraction to the “prize” may evaporate. The person who was pursued intensely during the rivalry may find themselves abandoned once the competition is over.

15.3.3 Jealousy

Jealousy is the emotional response to the perception that a valued relationship is threatened by a third party. It combines fear (of loss), anger (at the threat), and often shame (at not being enough to prevent the threat).

Jealousy can intensify attraction by making the stakes feel higher. The possibility of loss makes the relationship seem more valuable. The threat from a rival makes the partner seem more desirable. The pain of potential rejection makes the connection feel more urgent.

But jealousy is also destructive. Chronic jealousy erodes trust. Jealous behavior — monitoring, controlling, accusing — pushes partners away. The intensity that jealousy generates is not healthy attraction; it is anxious attachment dressed in passionate clothing.

15.3.4 The Forbidden and the Transgressive

Triangles often involve an element of the forbidden — desiring someone who is with someone else, being desired by someone who should not desire you. The forbidden quality adds intensity through the mechanisms we examined in Chapter 11: the edge dynamic, the thrill of transgression, the excitement of danger.

This is the mechanism behind the appeal of affairs, of “forbidden love,” of attractions that cross social boundaries. The prohibition intensifies the desire, not because the desired person is objectively more valuable, but because the prohibition adds the electricity of transgression.

15.4 Types of Triangles

15.4.1 The Rivalry Triangle

Two people desire the same third person. The rivalry between them intensifies both of their desires. The third person may be aware of the rivalry or not; either way, the rivalry shapes the dynamic.

Rivalry triangles are common in social contexts where multiple people are attracted to the same person. They can be relatively benign (mutual awareness of competition without hostility) or intensely destructive (active sabotage, manipulation, harm).

15.4.2 The Choice Triangle

One person is choosing between two others. This is the classic love triangle of literature and film. The person in the choosing position has structural power — they control the outcome. The two people being chosen between are in structurally subordinate positions, their fates dependent on the chooser’s decision.

Choice triangles generate intense emotion because they involve the possibility of rejection and the competition for scarce affection. They are also ethically complex. The chooser has a responsibility to be honest about the situation and to make decisions that minimize harm.

15.4.3 The Jealousy Triangle

A committed relationship is threatened by a third party — real or imagined. One partner’s jealousy shapes the dynamic, even if the third party is not actually a threat. The jealousy triangle is often more about the jealous person’s psychology than about any real triangle.

15.4.4 The Social Proof Triangle

A person’s attractiveness is amplified by the visible desire of others. This is not a triangle in the strict sense — the third parties may not be rivals or alternatives — but it operates through similar mechanisms. The social proof provided by others’ attention increases perceived value.

15.4.5 The Historical Triangle

A past relationship shapes a present one. The ex-partner, the former love, the person who came before — these historical figures can function as third points in present triangles. The comparison with the past, the shadow of what was, the fear of not measuring up — these dynamics are triangular even though the third point exists only in memory.

15.5 Navigating Triangles

15.5.1 Honesty

The most important principle for navigating triangles is honesty. If you are in a triangle, be honest about it — with yourself and with the other people involved. Deception about the existence of other parties, about the nature of your feelings, about your intentions — this is where triangles become genuinely harmful.

Honesty does not mean disclosing every detail. It means not lying, not misleading, not allowing people to operate under false assumptions about the situation they are in.

15.5.2 Clarity

Triangles thrive on ambiguity. When no one is clear about who wants whom, about what the situation is, about what might happen — the ambiguity generates the tension that fuels the triangle. Clarity resolves triangles, for better or worse.

If you want to exit a triangle, create clarity. State your feelings. Make your choice. End the ambiguity. The clarity may be painful in the short term, but it is less painful than prolonged uncertainty.

15.5.3 Responsibility

If you are the person being pursued by multiple people, you have a responsibility to manage the situation ethically. This means:

  • Not exploiting the competition for your own gratification
  • Not keeping people in suspense unnecessarily
  • Making decisions in a reasonable timeframe
  • Being honest with everyone involved
  • Taking responsibility for the emotional consequences of your choices

15.5.4 Self-Examination

If you find yourself repeatedly in triangles — always the rival, always the one choosing, always the one being competed over — examine why. Triangles can become patterns, and patterns reveal psychology. The person who is always the rival may be drawn to unavailable people. The person who is always the chooser may be avoiding commitment. The person who is always competed over may be using others’ desire as a substitute for self-worth.

15.6 Beyond Triangles

Triangles are a feature of the social landscape of attraction. They cannot be eliminated, but they can be navigated with awareness and ethics. The key is to recognize when a triangle is operating, to understand how it is shaping your feelings and behavior, and to act with honesty and responsibility.
