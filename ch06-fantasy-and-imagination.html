6  Fantasy and Imagination

6.1 The World Inside

Attraction happens as much in the mind as in the world. When you are attracted to someone, you do not merely perceive them; you imagine them. You imagine conversations you might have, experiences you might share, futures you might build. You imagine who you would be with them, how they would see you, what life would feel like in their presence.

This inner world of fantasy is not a distraction from “real” attraction. It is an essential component of it. Fantasy provides the emotional fuel that keeps attraction alive between encounters. It fills the gaps in knowledge with imagined possibilities. It transforms a person from a collection of observed facts into a character in an unfolding story.

6.2 The Nature of Fantasy

Fantasy, in the context of attraction, is the imaginative elaboration of a person beyond what is directly known. It includes:

  • Anticipatory fantasy: imagining future interactions, conversations, and experiences with the person
  • Interpretive fantasy: imagining what the person thinks, feels, and means by their words and actions
  • Narrative fantasy: constructing stories about who the person is, what their life has been, and what a relationship with them would be like
  • Self-fantasy: imagining who you would be in relation to this person — more confident, more desirable, more alive, more complete

Fantasy is not the same as delusion. Delusion is believing something false despite contrary evidence. Fantasy is entertaining possibilities without necessarily committing to their truth. The person deep in attraction fantasy may know, on some level, that their imaginings are exaggerated. But the fantasies feel good, feel meaningful, feel alive — so they continue.

6.3 Why Fantasy Matters for Attraction

6.3.1 Fantasy Creates Emotional Investment

The more you fantasize about someone, the more emotionally invested you become. Each fantasy is a small deposit of emotional energy. Over time, these deposits accumulate into significant attachment. You feel connected to the person not just because of what has happened between you, but because of everything you have imagined.

This is why people can develop intense attractions to people they have barely interacted with. The interactions may be minimal, but the fantasies are extensive. The emotional investment is real even if the relationship is mostly imaginary.

6.3.2 Fantasy Fills the Gaps

We never have complete information about another person. Fantasy supplies the missing pieces. When you do not know what someone is thinking, you imagine it. When you do not know their history, you construct it. When you do not know their feelings, you project them.

The less information available, the more room fantasy has to operate. This is one reason mystery is so powerful in attraction: mystery creates information gaps that fantasy eagerly fills. The mysterious person becomes a screen onto which elaborate fantasies can be projected.

6.3.3 Fantasy Generates Desire

Fantasy intensifies desire by rehearsing satisfaction. When you imagine being with someone, you experience a preview of the pleasure, connection, or fulfillment that being with them might bring. This preview generates wanting. The imagined satisfaction creates hunger for the real thing.

This is the mechanism behind the common experience of desire intensifying during absence. When the person is not present, fantasy operates freely. When they return, reality competes with fantasy — and reality rarely measures up to what imagination has constructed.

6.3.4 Fantasy Shapes Perception

Fantasy does not just add to perception; it alters it. When you have fantasized extensively about someone, you perceive them through the lens of those fantasies. You notice things that confirm your fantasies and overlook things that contradict them. The person becomes, in your perception, more like your fantasy of them.

This is why people in the throes of attraction often seem blind to obvious flaws or red flags. They are not stupid; they are perceiving through a fantasy-filtered lens that highlights what fits the fantasy and obscures what does not.

6.4 The Architecture of Attraction Fantasy

6.4.1 The Romantic Narrative

The most common form of attraction fantasy is the romantic narrative: the story of how you met, how you connected, how you overcame obstacles, how you came together. This narrative may be partly true, but it is always partly constructed. Events are selected, emphasized, and interpreted to fit a satisfying story arc.

The romantic narrative serves an important function: it gives meaning to attraction. Raw attraction — “I want this person” — feels incomplete without a story about why you want them, what it means, where it is going. The narrative provides that meaning.

6.4.2 The Transformation Fantasy

Many attraction fantasies center on transformation: the belief that being with this person will change you, heal you, complete you, or unlock your potential. The beloved is imagined as the key to becoming who you are meant to be.

This fantasy is powerful because it connects attraction to the deep human desire for growth and wholeness. But it is also dangerous. It places an impossible burden on the other person — the burden of being your salvation. And it sets up inevitable disappointment when the transformation does not occur.

6.4.3 The Recognition Fantasy

The recognition fantasy is the belief that this person sees you as you truly are — perhaps for the first time. They understand you. They get you. They recognize your worth, your depth, your uniqueness in a way that others have not.

This fantasy is especially powerful for people who feel unseen or misunderstood in their ordinary lives. The attraction is not just to the person but to the experience of being seen. The beloved becomes a mirror that reflects back an idealized image of the self.

6.4.4 The Rescue Fantasy

The rescue fantasy imagines the beloved as someone who will save you — from loneliness, from boredom, from a difficult life situation, from yourself. Alternatively, it imagines you as the rescuer, saving the beloved from their difficulties.

Rescue fantasies are common in attractions that form during periods of vulnerability. They provide hope and motivation, but they also create dependency. The attraction is sustained by need rather than by genuine connection.

6.5 The Dangers of Fantasy-Driven Attraction

Reality will eventually intrude. Fantasy-driven attraction is inherently unstable. Eventually, enough reality accumulates that the fantasy can no longer be sustained. The crash can be devastating, especially if significant emotional investment has been made.

Fantasy prevents genuine knowing. When you are relating primarily to your fantasy of someone, you are not relating to them. You are having a relationship with your own imagination. This is lonely, even when it feels exciting.

Fantasy enables self-deception. People can maintain fantasy-driven attractions for years by selectively attending to confirming evidence and dismissing disconfirming evidence. They are not lying to others; they are lying to themselves.

Fantasy can become addictive. The emotional high of fantasy-driven attraction can become a pattern. Some people move from one intense fantasy-attraction to another, never staying long enough for reality to catch up. They are addicted to the fantasy phase and incapable of the reality phase.

6.6 Healthy Fantasy

Fantasy is not inherently pathological. It becomes problematic only when it substitutes for reality rather than complementing it. Healthy fantasy in attraction has several characteristics:

It is recognized as fantasy. The person knows they are imagining possibilities, not perceiving certainties. They hold their fantasies lightly.

It is tested against reality. Fantasies are treated as hypotheses to be checked, not as truths to be defended. When reality contradicts fantasy, the fantasy is adjusted.

It does not replace engagement. Fantasy enriches the relationship; it does not replace actual interaction. The person continues to show up, communicate, and connect in reality.

It is shared. In an established relationship, fantasies can be shared: “I was imagining us doing this together.” Shared fantasy builds intimacy. Private fantasy, when it becomes the primary relationship, undermines it.

6.7 From Inner Theater to Outer Dynamics

Fantasy and projection constitute the inner theater of attraction — the psychological processes that make another person emotionally significant. But attraction also involves outer dynamics: the rhythms of approach and withdrawal, the management of distance and availability, the creation and release of tension. These are the subjects of Part III, beginning with mystery.
